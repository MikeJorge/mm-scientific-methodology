How to read this methodology¶

The document provides an accessible account of the scientific-development method and a separately identifiable reconstruction record. Readers should follow the pathway appropriate to the question they need to verify; no pathway substitutes for the controlling source evidence.

  • Scientific readers: identify the question, declared intended use, model assumptions, verification evidence, validation evidence, and uncertainty limits.
  • Software engineers and architects: examine authority, architecture, versioned interfaces, the software development life cycle (SDLC), tests, and release readback.
  • Auditors: trace a claim to its Architecture requirement record (AR), Manual requirement record (DM), applicability rule, responsible actor, verification evidence, and terminal decision.
  • Interdisciplinary reviewers: assess the method’s contribution, transferability, scientific credibility, reproducibility, and explicitly stated limitations.

The principal narrative explains methodological decisions. The Audit and Traceability collection supplies the certified identifiers, requirement registers, provenance distinctions, and limits necessary to inspect the evidence behind those explanations.

1. Purpose¶

The Module Minimum (MM) scientific software development and assurance methodology describes how a multidisciplinary computational research system is specified, developed, examined, integrated, and released under explicit evidence and authority constraints. MHSMM is the controlled identifier for the project economic and risk modeling system. The admitted historical glossary does not establish a reliable long-form expansion of the identifier. The present document describes the engineering method, not an independently validated or installable model runtime.

The methodological baseline draws on the certified Scientific Execution Architecture, version 1.0.2; the Scientific Software Development and Assurance Manual, version 1.0.1 release candidate 3 (RC3); the Project Evidence Base, version 1.0.0; and separately controlled execution-adapter, harness, and technical-reference records. Guidance from the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA), including software assurance and independent verification and validation (IV&V), informs selected practices. Use of that guidance does not establish NASA compliance, endorsement, or certification.

The research system combines geospatial transformations, mechanistic plant and forage modeling, animal-response models, stochastic economic analysis, data engineering, and uncertainty assessment. A correct implementation can still answer an incorrectly posed scientific question. Assurance must therefore examine the estimand, intended use, assumptions, model structure, data semantics, interface contracts, controls, and decision criteria in addition to source code and tests.

2. The governing assurance question¶

Scientific credibility requires evidence for two distinct questions: whether the implemented system satisfies its specified requirements and whether those requirements and the implemented system are appropriate for the stated scientific use.

  1. Was the system built correctly?
    Does the implementation conform to the specified requirements, architecture, interfaces, contracts, numerical rules, security constraints, and release criteria?
  2. Was the correct system built?
    Do the requirements, architecture, controls, tests, and acceptance criteria represent the intended scientific use and the actual scientific question?

Verification examines conformity to specified requirements and design expectations; validation examines suitability for intended use under the declared operating conditions. NASA software-engineering and IV&V guidance applies these questions across requirements, architecture, implementation, interfaces, and tests. The project cites this guidance as a methodological source, not as evidence that NASA reviewed the MM implementation [R02], [EXT-NASA-SWE067], [EXT-NASA-IVV], [EXT-NASA-SA].

For a given development Gate, verification must identify the specification and the objective evidence demonstrating that its obligations were met.

IMPLEMENTATION_CONFORMS_TO_REQUIREMENTS
        does not imply
REQUIREMENTS_REPRESENT_THE_RIGHT_SCIENTIFIC_SYSTEM

Validation must independently identify the scientific purpose, applicable domain, and evidence supporting the relevance of the outputs to that purpose.

REPRODUCIBLE_EXECUTION
        does not imply
SCIENTIFIC_VALIDITY

Successful execution, a passing software test, or byte-for-byte reproducibility does not by itself establish scientific validity. A positive terminal determination requires the evidence prescribed for both implementation correctness and intended-use adequacy, insofar as each is applicable to the Gate.

3.2 Evidence classification¶

Material statements must be classified according to their evidentiary basis. The classification distinguishes direct observations, derived analyses, historical records, and statements not established by available evidence.

  • DIRECT: measured or read directly from admitted bytes, a runtime observation, or an authoritative source;
  • BOUND: established through an exact cryptographic or referential binding to a DIRECT authority;
  • DERIVED: logically derived from admitted predicates or source relationships;
  • HISTORICAL: valid as a preserved prior observation but not freshly re-measured;
  • NOT_ESTABLISHED: not proven by the available evidence.

A derived analysis is not a newly observed measurement. A historical PASS does not establish current execution authority, and a historical HOLD must not be erased by a later unrelated success. Where the evidence is insufficient, the document reports that limitation rather than replacing it with an assumption.

3.3 Project Source discipline¶

Project Sources preserve reusable, adjudicated knowledge, including governing authorities and durable terminal decisions. They are distinct from event logs, exploratory attempts, and transient tool output. The latter remain part of the provenance record but do not automatically enter the active scientific baseline.

4. Architecture of the MM scientific system¶

The architecture separates domain models and the contracts through which they exchange data from the governance mechanisms used to authorize, observe, and assess their execution. This separation permits scientific modules to evolve without silently changing the authority or semantics of another component.

4.2 Minimum inter-module contract¶

Every material producer-to-consumer interface requires an explicit contract. The minimum specification addresses the following elements, with nonapplicability justified where appropriate.

  • schema and semantic version;
  • variable definition and scientific meaning;
  • units and dimensional conventions;
  • valid domain/ranges and biological or physical constraints;
  • missingness, null semantics, and forbidden implicit defaults;
  • spatial reference, including coordinate reference system (CRS), coordinate operation where material, axis semantics, grid/affine transform, resolution, extent, support, mask, and resampling rules;
  • temporal reference, aggregation window, calendar, and time-zone semantics;
  • provenance, acquisition/version identity, transformations, and evidence links;
  • compatibility/deprecation rules;
  • positive, negative, boundary, and off-nominal contract tests.

6. Gate-governed scientific development¶

A Gate is a versioned decision and execution-control boundary. It identifies the authorized inputs, controlling records, responsible actors, qualified capabilities, decision rules, permitted effects, and evidence needed to determine its terminal state.

  • exact input authorities;
  • scientific question and intended decision;
  • actors and roles;
  • capabilities;
  • preconditions and predicates;
  • permitted side effects and budgets;
  • objective evidence requirements;
  • verification and validation methods;
  • terminal classes;
  • independent readback/review;
  • next allowed state;
  • STOP=true.

A tool reporting success establishes at most that a specified operation returned successfully. Scientific acceptance requires a separate examination of the operation's identity, output, limitations, and the evidence required by the governing Gate.

6.2 Total and exclusive terminal partition¶

Before consequential execution, the admissible input and outcome space must be divided into terminal classes that are exhaustive and mutually exclusive. The formal requirement is stated below.

for every admissible state s:
    exactly one terminal class C_i(s) is true

The terminal partition must cover valid outcomes as well as missing observations, invalid inputs, null states, empty or partial spatial support, control failure, transport failure, and unrelated exceptions. Where an executable partition check is feasible, an informal assertion of completeness is insufficient.

6.3 Fail-closed rule¶

A Gate enters HOLD when the available evidence is missing, stale, contradictory, ambiguous, or otherwise inadequate to establish the required predicate. The system does not infer PASS or permission from an absence of recorded failure.

7. Scientific software development lifecycle¶

The development process is organized around progressively stronger evidence, beginning with a defensible scientific question and ending with an independently inspected release state. Its twelve operational phases, numbered 0 through 11, are distinct from the nine visual stages in Figure 1. The figure summarizes major evidence-producing activities; it is not a one-to-one phase map and does not authorize omission of any phase.

Relationship to Figure 1. The figure contains nine schematic stages: scientific need, requirements, architecture, detailed design, implementation, verification, validation, independent readback and assurance, and freeze or release candidate. Section 7 describes twelve operational phases, numbered 0 to 11. The additional detailed topics include integrated verification and validation, uncertainty and sensitivity analysis, controlled release, independent readback, and freeze or certification. These texts describe different levels of detail; no certified one-to-one correspondence is claimed. The full operational phases remain applicable regardless of what is condensed in the illustration.

Phase 0. Scientific need and intended use¶

The project first specifies the question the scientific software must answer, its intended users and decisions, and the physical and inferential domain in which its output is expected to be meaningful. The record identifies the proposed estimand and the assumptions required to interpret it.

  • scientific question;
  • intended use and decision context;
  • estimand or target quantity;
  • domain of validity;
  • stakeholders/users;
  • consequences of false positive, false negative, and unsupported outputs;
  • materiality and risk.

The validation question at this stage is whether the proposed computational problem corresponds to the intended scientific decision. A technically solvable problem is not necessarily the appropriate one.

Phase 1. Requirements engineering¶

Requirements must be uniquely identifiable, traceable to a justified purpose, sufficiently atomic to test, and explicit about scientific meaning, interfaces, quality attributes, and acceptance criteria. Requirement statements must distinguish necessary behavior from implementation convenience.

  • correct relative to the scientific need;
  • complete for the declared scope;
  • internally consistent;
  • unambiguous;
  • traceable upward to scientific/system needs;
  • traceable downward to architecture/design/code/tests;
  • individually verifiable or otherwise explicitly justified;
  • testable where testability is required.

Implementability alone does not establish that a requirement is necessary, scientifically appropriate, or sufficiently specified. Requirements require substantive review before implementation.

Phase 2. Architecture and interface design¶

Architectural design assigns requirements to bounded scientific modules, controlled interfaces, data contracts, and execution responsibilities. Reviewers examine both whether the architecture can satisfy the specification and whether its boundaries preserve the scientific and evidentiary assumptions on which that specification depends.

  • verification: does the architecture satisfy the baselined requirements?;
  • validation: is the architecture suitable for the intended scientific use and credible under realistic operating/scientific conditions?

Changes to a material cross-module relationship require a versioned interface and an impact assessment covering producers, consumers, units, missing-value semantics, and the evidence needed to requalify affected behavior.

Phase 3. Detailed design and algorithm specification¶

Detailed design identifies the numerical and statistical methods, their domains of validity, assumptions, algorithms, error controls, input and output representations, and the specific evidence by which implementation will be examined. Alternative methods must not be treated as interchangeable without justification.

  • algorithm and mathematical formulation;
  • assumptions and regularity conditions;
  • numerical method and tolerances;
  • failure modes;
  • data structures and schemas;
  • interfaces and invariants;
  • precision/convergence requirements;
  • performance/resource assumptions where material;
  • negative and boundary behavior;
  • evidence that the design remains traceable to requirements.

Phase 4. Implementation¶

Implementation must conform to the reviewed architecture and design. Explicit dependencies, controlled configuration, reproducible computational environments, boundary-aware error handling, and tests for scientifically significant operations are part of the implementation record.

  • version-controlled exact source;
  • deterministic or explicitly characterized build process;
  • dependency/environment identity;
  • code review/diff review;
  • static analysis where applicable;
  • unit tests and negative fixtures;
  • no undocumented shared mutable state;
  • no silent fallback that changes scientific semantics;
  • explicit treatment of errors and unknown states.

Phase 5. Verification¶

Verification compares software artifacts with their governing requirements. Depending on the component, evidence may comprise static inspection, automated unit tests, interface tests, negative and boundary cases, regression comparisons, or exact-byte reconstruction.

  • static analysis;
  • peer inspection;
  • unit and integration testing;
  • contract tests;
  • property/invariant checks;
  • mutation testing;
  • numerical regression tests;
  • exact-byte identity/readback;
  • independent oracle comparison;
  • off-nominal and boundary tests.

Every applicable requirement must have a stated verification method and identifiable objective evidence. A general assertion that testing was performed does not demonstrate coverage.

Phase 6. Validation¶

Validation concerns the scientific appropriateness of an implemented system for its stated use. It may require comparison with observations, independent models, known physical limits, external reference cases, or domain-specific acceptance criteria established before results are examined.

  • comparison with analytical solutions;
  • comparison with trusted benchmark/reference implementations;
  • reproduction of accepted baselines;
  • comparison against independent empirical observations;
  • external or withheld validation datasets;
  • sensitivity and robustness analysis;
  • domain-expert review;
  • operationally representative scenarios;
  • evaluation of model-form limitations and boundary conditions.

Unit-test success is necessary for many implementations but cannot substitute for evidence that the modeled phenomena, assumptions, and outputs are suitable for the scientific question.

Phase 7. Integration verification and validation¶

Components that pass separate tests can fail when coupled. Integrated verification and validation therefore examine contract compatibility, data lineage, transformations, synchronization, uncertainty propagation, and system-level behavior against declared acceptance criteria.

  • contract compatibility;
  • unit consistency;
  • temporal/spatial support consistency;
  • provenance continuity;
  • cross-module state consistency;
  • propagation of missingness and uncertainty;
  • interface error handling;
  • scenario reproducibility;
  • integrated scientific behavior against independent expectations.

Phase 8. Uncertainty, sensitivity, and robustness¶

Uncertainty assessment separates stochastic variability, parameter uncertainty, structural assumptions, measurement error, numerical approximation, and spatial or temporal support effects. These sources differ in interpretation and should not be combined without an explicit uncertainty model.

  • parameter uncertainty;
  • stochastic variability;
  • measurement uncertainty;
  • structural/model-form uncertainty;
  • numerical error;
  • spatial support/resolution uncertainty;
  • scenario uncertainty.

The sensitivity design must precede the simulation. It specifies input factors, ranges and dependencies, sampling methods, estimators, random-stream management, diagnostic criteria, and output measures that address the intended decision.

Phase 9. Controlled release¶

A candidate release is admitted only when requirements, implementation checks, scientific validation evidence, interface compatibility, and provenance satisfy the predetermined criteria. Apparent correctness cannot compensate for a missing mandatory record.

Phase 10. Independent readback and assurance¶

An independent reviewer reconstructs decisive identities and outcomes from the declared evidence. The actor who made an authorized change cannot rely on that act as independent certification of its correctness.

Phase 11. Freeze / certification¶

For certified project authorities, the durable release pattern separates a content commit, an independently verified remote readback, and a subsequent certification commit. Each stage has its own identity and recorded conditions; the original notation is retained in the controlled specification.

  1. Content Commit A establishes recoverable content.
  2. Complete readback/reconstruction verifies exact persisted bytes.
  3. Certification Commit B records the zero-mismatch result.

A frozen or certified source is not an authorization to run scientific workloads. Execution permission must be issued for the particular Gate, environment, inputs, and permitted effects.

8. Verification, validation, and IV&V-inspired completeness checks¶

For a material change, reviewers must examine the completeness and scientific relevance of requirements, architecture, implementation, tests, data contracts, and the stated intended use. The checks below specify the minimum questions to answer.

8.1 Requirements¶

  • Are all in-scope scientific/system requirements represented?
  • Are requirements correct, complete, consistent, traceable, readable, and testable?
  • Are constraints, interfaces, error states, and boundary conditions explicit?
  • Can each requirement be verified without reinterpretation after observing results?
  • Are requirements that cannot be directly tested still objectively assessable by analysis/review?

8.2 Architecture¶

  • Does every in-scope requirement map to one or more architectural elements?
  • Are architecture relationships and interfaces correct, consistent, complete, and traceable?
  • Can the architecture support the intended scientific use?
  • Are undesired interactions and failure propagation considered?
  • Is each authoritative boundary explicit?

8.3 Detailed design¶

  • Is design traceable to requirements and architecture?
  • Are interfaces internally consistent and externally complete?
  • Are numerical and statistical assumptions explicit?
  • Are all material negative/off-nominal states represented?

8.4 Implementation¶

  • Does code preserve architecture, contracts, and requirements?
  • Are all invariants enforced by typed/executable predicates where feasible?
  • Does the implementation reject invalid states rather than normalize them silently?
  • Are exact versions/dependencies/build inputs recorded?

8.5 Testing¶

  • Does every material requirement have adequate test or analysis coverage?
  • Are tests correct and complete relative to the requirement set?
  • Do negative fixtures target known and plausible defect classes?
  • Are tests independent enough to detect common-mode errors?
  • Are acceptance criteria defined before observing the candidate?
  • Are nominal, boundary, off-nominal, robustness, and failure-recovery cases included as applicable?

8.6 Acceptance¶

Acceptance depends on the quality and completeness of the evidence needed to substantiate the claim. Documentary completeness is therefore a substantive assurance criterion, not merely a final administrative step.

10. Execution classes and authorization boundary¶

Execution activities are classified by their capacity to alter project state or external systems. Read-only examination, local nonproductive tests, protected scientific computation, and production operations have different authority and evidence requirements.

12. Harness reliability profile¶

The Harness Reliability Profile constrains mutable execution through explicit attempts, resources, tool capabilities, retry rules, side-effect allowances, and readback requirements. Its status must be assessed together with, rather than in place of, the governing Gate contract.

  1. one-shot execution (MAX_ATTEMPTS=1);
  2. no automatic retry;
  3. no implicit agent recursion or self-relaunch;
  4. typed process results;
  5. exact materialized-byte identity;
  6. observation before adjudication;
  7. total/exclusive terminal partition;
  8. resource bounds and descendant cleanup;
  9. filesystem least privilege;
  10. network least privilege;
  11. explicit UID/GID/capability boundary;
  12. numeric side-effect budgets;
  13. atomic evidence lifecycle;
  14. independent readback;
  15. explicit STOP=true;
  16. regression closure for each discovered harness defect.

A harness can enforce declared restrictions but cannot supply the missing authority to run a scientific task. Its successful operation must not be confused with the validity of the task's scientific assumptions.

13. Data engineering, metadata, provenance, and evidence¶

13.1 Computational contract versus catalog metadata¶

A computational data contract defines machine-actionable semantics required by the producing and consuming scientific modules. Catalog metadata describes the discovery and reuse of the resulting datasets and services. The two serve different purposes and are examined separately.

  • computational data contracts: exact interface semantics needed for execution;
  • DCAT: dataset/service discovery;
  • PROV-O: provenance/derivation relations;
  • SHACL: graph constraint validation;
  • DQV: data-quality metadata;
  • Project Evidence Base: certified scientific/technical claims, decisions, conflicts, gaps, and evidence relations.

The World Wide Web Consortium (W3C) provides standards and vocabularies for structured data, including the Resource Description Framework (RDF). The Data Catalog Vocabulary (DCAT), Provenance Ontology (PROV-O), Shapes Constraint Language (SHACL), and Data Quality Vocabulary (DQV) support distinct aspects of interoperability. Their use does not replace an executable data contract or, without further evidence, establish compliance with a W3C recommendation [R29].

13.2 Data-quality evidence¶

A material dataset or transformation needs a documented quality profile identifying the source, data contract, checks performed, observed results, unsupported states, and the evidence supporting use in subsequent computation.

  • schema validity;
  • units and ranges;
  • missingness/null semantics;
  • duplicate/key checks where relevant;
  • spatial/temporal coverage;
  • CRS/grid identity where relevant;
  • provenance and source version;
  • transformation lineage;
  • freshness where material;
  • required-versus-executed check equality.

The label CERTIFIED is not self-authenticating. A data-quality assessment must identify the executed tests, their results, and the authority against which those results were evaluated.

14. Geospatial and GEE assurance¶

Geospatial assurance requires explicit treatment of coordinate reference systems (CRSs), datum transformations, grid alignment, resolution, spatial support, masks, resampling, and area semantics. Official Google Earth Engine documentation informs platform-specific behavior and must be assessed for version and freshness when material to a decision [R18]–[R21].

  • CRS definition;
  • coordinate operation/datum transformation;
  • axis semantics;
  • affine/grid transform;
  • pixel origin/alignment;
  • nominal resolution/scale;
  • spatial extent and target support;
  • observable support/mask;
  • resampling/aggregation semantics;
  • geodesic versus planar area conventions;
  • task/export identity and receipt.

An unobserved or masked value must not be interpreted as numerical zero without a justified rule. Empty support, partial coverage, complete coverage, and invalid geometry are distinct scientific states with potentially different terminal outcomes.

15. Mathematical and numerical assurance¶

Numerical assurance addresses well-posedness, conditioning, algorithmic stability, rounding behavior, convergence, and error budgets. Where applicable, the record draws on Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) floating-point standards and guidance of the Joint Committee for Guides in Metrology (JCGM), including the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement (GUM), without claiming external certification [R22]–[R25].

  • well-posed problem and domain of validity;
  • assumptions/regularity conditions;
  • conditioning and sensitivity;
  • algorithmic stability;
  • finite-precision behavior;
  • convergence criterion and stopping rule;
  • justified tolerance/error bound linked to the scientific decision;
  • comparison against analytical/trusted reference results where feasible;
  • negative/boundary behavior;
  • exact artifact identity.

16. Statistical, geostatistical, Monte Carlo, and UQ assurance¶

Statistical and geostatistical assurance requires a prospectively specified design of experiments (DOE), explicit sampling and dependence assumptions, justified uncertainty quantification (UQ), and fit-for-purpose validation. The assessment should record estimators, random-stream policy, convergence and precision diagnostics, and safeguards against spatial or temporal data leakage [R11], [R23]–[R28].

  • sampling design;
  • probability distributions and parameterization;
  • truncation/bounds;
  • dependence/correlation structure;
  • random seed and independent stream strategy;
  • convergence/precision target;
  • estimator and sample-size design;
  • validation design;
  • spatial/temporal cross-validation that avoids leakage;
  • uncertainty category being propagated or excluded;
  • decision outputs and interpretation.

An iteration count does not establish adequate Monte Carlo precision. Numerical uncertainty and stochastic estimation error must be assessed against the magnitude and consequence of the intended scientific decision.

17. Scientific model credibility¶

NASA Handbook 7009B is cited as a source on model and simulation credibility. The project uses its methodological distinctions when discussing intended use, evidence, limitations, and credibility assessment, without claiming NASA certification or a NASA review of MM [R04].

  • modular model/system decomposition;
  • explicit interfaces and interactions among submodels;
  • credibility of component and integrated models;
  • input pedigree/provenance and data quality;
  • uncertainty characterization;
  • robustness of results;
  • credibility considerations for AI-assisted modeling/simulation.

Credibility is assessed for each affected model component and for the coupled system. Confidence in a component cannot automatically be transferred to an integrated system with new interfaces, assumptions, or sources of uncertainty.

18. Configuration management, deterministic packaging, and exact-byte authority¶

18.1 Configuration identity¶

A reproducible scientific result must be associated with exact, recoverable identities for relevant code, data, configurations, models, dependencies, and interface contracts. A filename, timestamp, or human-readable version label is insufficient as a sole identity.

18.2 Deterministic packaging¶

When byte identity is a release property, the packaging procedure controls member ordering, timestamp normalization, compression method, platform metadata, file modes, directory-entry behavior, and checksum coverage. When deterministic double-build is required, both artifacts and their exact-byte comparison must remain independently inspectable.

18.3 Readback¶

After persistence, the reviewer retrieves the artifact from the authoritative destination and compares its content with the frozen expected identity. A successful upload message is not proof of byte equality; a material mismatch results in HOLD.

18.4 Living external-source control¶

Static standards and evolving online guidance have different change-control requirements. A living source must be identified by a documented access state and reevaluated when a change could affect a scientific requirement or platform assumption.

  • explicit source URL/identifier;
  • access date or snapshot identity;
  • freshness review;
  • impact analysis against affected requirements/contracts;
  • prospective versioning before the source changes an MM rule.

This principle is established in the historical Technical Reference, section 17.1, and in certified Manual requirement DM-0195. A revised online source does not amend a frozen project obligation without an authorized impact review and new version.

The project references International Organization for Standardization (ISO), International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC), and IEEE standards using public bibliographic metadata unless licensed normative text is separately admitted. Citations inform engineering rationale; the project's certified records determine applicable local obligations.

19. Independent review, mutation testing, and adversarial assurance¶

Independent assurance examines not only the software implementation but also whether the tests, decision predicates, and review process can detect scientifically significant failures.

19.1 Mutation testing¶

Mutation testing deliberately introduces specified defects in a controlled candidate or validator to determine whether its assurance tests reject them. The mutation set must reflect the invariants material to the decision.

  • canonical requirements/source;
  • executable resolver or decision logic;
  • referential/traceability bindings;
  • schema/type invariants;
  • compatibility constraints;
  • authority hashes;
  • negative paths and failure classifications.

A material defect that escapes the prescribed mutation tests prevents a positive assurance determination until the test or implementation deficiency is resolved prospectively.

19.2 Common-mode protection¶

An independent oracle should implement the expected decision semantics without delegating to the production resolver under examination. Reviewers should reconstruct decisive evidence from admitted sources instead of relying exclusively on the producer's summary.

19.3 Evidence-completeness lesson from Attempt004¶

The Attempt004 record illustrates a documentary evidence failure, not a demonstrated new runtime defect. Its final independent review passed checks 1 through 44 within the static review scope and held check 45 because the sealed negative-test matrix did not make all mandatory negative classes independently inspectable. The original findings and candidate identities remain unchanged.

The broader lesson is that the evidence needed to assess a negative claim must be defined before execution and supplied in a form that permits independent reconstruction.

IMPLEMENTATION_GUARDS_APPEAR_CORRECT
        does not imply
MANDATORY_ASSURANCE_EVIDENCE_IS_COMPLETE

If a Gate requires negative tests, the actual fixtures, test definitions, expected outcomes, and coverage record belong to the evidence package. A statement that the tests passed cannot replace the evidence that would allow their adequacy to be assessed.

22. Claim discipline for scientific publications¶

23. Publication-facing reproducibility profile¶

This section describes publication-facing reporting practices rather than additional certified MM requirements. Its purpose is to make the methodology intelligible and inspectable by readers across computational science and applied research disciplines.

23.1 Nature Computational Science alignment¶

Nature Computational Science considers contributions in computational methods, mathematical modeling, and tools that advance research across scientific disciplines. An MM manuscript could be within this broad subject area only if the actual contribution, evidence, generality, and scientific significance satisfy the journal's editorial criteria [EXT-NCS-AIMS]. This documentation is not itself a journal submission.

Nature Portfolio emphasizes access to the code, data, and protocols needed to examine claims, with explicit disclosure of restrictions. Manuscripts that depend centrally on custom code may require code-availability statements and material for reviewer inspection [EXT-NCS-POLICY], [EXT-NATURE-CODE]. Whether those policies apply to a particular submission must be assessed against its article type and claims.

23.2 Computational reproducibility¶

Computational reproducibility requires enough information to identify the scientific question, source material, software and dependencies, parameterization, execution procedure, and the checks that distinguish agreement from a fortuitous result.

  • evidence supporting result validity, such as convergence tests or comparison with analytical/trusted standard approaches;
  • code availability;
  • installation/running documentation;
  • tests and use examples;
  • explicit software versions and input files where relevant [EXT-NATURE-COMP-REPRO].

Versioned documentation, source identity, testability, and result validation contribute to the evidence needed for scientific review. Their value lies in the questions they enable readers to verify, not in repository presentation alone.

23.3 Editorial non-claim¶

Neither a favorable scope interpretation nor a journal's citation metric predicts acceptance. Evaluation depends on the final manuscript, the originality and significance of its contribution, and the adequacy of the supporting scientific evidence.

24. Two-article role of this methodology¶

The project maintains two distinct editorial purposes. One manuscript concerns the model as a reusable scientific instrument and the evidence supporting its reproducibility and trustworthiness. The other concerns substantive agricultural, economic, and environmental findings that may be obtained through separately authorized model execution.

Research software reuse is discussed using the principles of findability, accessibility, interoperability, and reusability (FAIR). The corresponding FAIR Principles for Research Software (FAIR4RS) are cited as reporting and reuse guidance; neither acronym is an external certification.

  • RAFE-1 / Data- or Resource-oriented paper: the MM/MHSMM scientific instrument, reusable computational representation, FAIR/provenance layer, reproducibility, certified Architecture/Manual, Gate model, assurance, readback, and release/reuse;
  • RAFE-2 / Research paper: substantive agricultural, economic, environmental, spatial, uncertainty, and land-use knowledge obtained from qualified scientific execution.

The internal markers RAFE-1 and RAFE-2 identify those two manuscript tracks; they are project routing codes, not external journal article categories. This methodological document primarily supports the first track. The second should cite only the assurance details necessary to support its substantive inferences and avoid repeating the full engineering account.

30.3 External-source use and freshness statement¶

Standards, scientific literature, platform manuals, and journal policies inform the project's engineering choices and reporting practices. The certified MM authorities remain the operative sources for project-specific obligations.

Living online guidance is identified by access date and relevant version or snapshot when those properties are available. Changes to that guidance require a fresh impact assessment before they affect a certified project requirement. Referencing NASA, ISO, IEC, IEEE, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), the W3C, the FAIR Principles for Research Software (FAIR4RS), Google, the Joint Committee for Guides in Metrology, R Core, or Nature Portfolio does not constitute external compliance or institutional endorsement.

The source and reference register provides the full controlled citation inventory, including the distinction between historical sources and later contextual additions. Its source status is evidence about a reference's identity, not certification of compliance.
